Module 1 · Step 3 of 6

Dimensions, measures, and the pill colors

Tableau Desktop · 15 min

Tableau splits every field into dimensions (categories that slice the data) and measures (quantities that get aggregated). The horizontal line in the data pane is the divider.

  1. Drag college to Rows.
  2. Drag student_id to Columns.
  3. Right-click that pill → Measure → Count (Distinct).

You get a bar chart, unasked. Tableau chose bars because you gave it one dimension and one measure — the default encoding for comparing a quantity across categories.

Blue and green

Blue pills are discrete: they draw headers and split the view into panes. Green pills are continuous: they draw axes.

  • Drag cohort_year to Columns. It arrives green, as an axis, because it's a number.
  • Right-click it → Discrete. It turns blue and becomes three column headers.

Which is right depends on your question. As a continuous axis, 2020–2022 implies a measurable span. As discrete headers, they're three labelled groups. For three cohorts, discrete reads better.

Module 1 · Step 6 of 6

The ethics of the axis

Tableau Desktop · 20 min · graded deliverable

This is the piece you submit. You'll build the same chart twice — once honestly, once deceptively — and defend the difference.

  1. New worksheet. college to Columns.
  2. is_graduated to Rows, set to Average. That gives you a graduation rate between 0 and 1.
  3. Screenshot this. It's your honest chart.
  4. Right-click the vertical axis → Edit Axis.
  5. Untick Include zero, then fix the range to start just below your lowest bar.
  6. Screenshot again.

Two charts, identical underlying numbers. The second makes a few percentage points look like a chasm.

The point

Bar length is the encoding in a bar chart. Cutting the baseline breaks the relationship between what the reader measures with their eye and what the data says. A line chart tracking change over time is a different case — truncation there is often defensible, and knowing why is the actual skill.

Module 2 · Step 2 of 8

Relationships versus joins

Tableau Desktop · 20 min

Connect mvu_student_dim.csv, then drag mvu_enrollment_fact.csv onto the canvas beside it.

Tableau draws a flexible line — a relationship. Set it to student_id = student_id.

Run the comparison

On a worksheet, put student_id on Text as Count (Distinct). Note the number.

Now go back, delete the relationship, and rebuild it as a physical inner join (double-click a table to open the physical layer). Check the same number again.

What just happened

A relationship keeps each table at its own grain and aggregates each one correctly for whatever you put in the view. A physical join flattens both tables into one wide table at the enrolment grain, so every student is repeated once per course they took. Counts, averages and totals all inflate.

Record both numbers in your memo. This comparison is worth marks on its own.

Module 2 · Step 5 of 8

Find and fix three quality problems

Tableau Desktop · 30 min

Your exercise requires at least three documented data-quality conditions. This data has more than three; here are the ones worth writing up.

1 — Inconsistent categories

sex holds eight spellings of two values. residency holds eleven spellings of three. Any filter or group-by splits the same people across multiple buckets.

Keep the original field and build a cleaned one beside it:

UPPER(TRIM([sex]))

That collapses spacing and case but not wording, so finish with an alias or a group to fold MALE into M.

2 — Whitespace in a join key

program_code and campus_code carry leading and trailing spaces on some rows. Invisible on screen, fatal to a join — "CSCI " does not match "CSCI".

UPPER(TRIM([program_code]))

3 — Numbers stored as text, and impossible values

undergrad_gpa contains blanks and N/A, which forces the whole field to string. Change the type to Number (decimal) and the non-numeric entries become null. Then handle the values above 4.0:

IF [undergrad_gpa] > 4.0 THEN NULL ELSE [undergrad_gpa] END
Document before and after

For each issue your memo needs: the affected field, how it would distort a filter or calculation, what you did, and the counts either side of the fix. "I cleaned the data" earns nothing.

Module 2 · Step 6 of 8

Deduplicate — in the right order

Tableau Desktop · 15 min

The union gave you 1,413 rows but not 1,413 students. The 2021 file contains repeats — some exact, some differing only in the capitalisation of the name.

  1. Standardise the name field first: UPPER(TRIM([student_name])).
  2. Then remove duplicates — or aggregate to one row per student_id.
Order of operations

Deduplicate before standardising and the capitalisation variants survive, because "GARCIA, MARIA A." and "Garcia, Maria A." are different strings. Clean first, then dedupe. This ordering bug is quiet, common, and produces a number that looks almost right.

Module 2 · Step 7 of 8

Check for join fan-out

Tableau Desktop · 15 min

Your exercise asks you to investigate whether a join duplicated rows through one-to-many cardinality. The aid data is where it happens.

financial_aid.xlsx has multiple rows per student — one per aid year per aid type. Join it directly to your student table and every student is repeated once per award. Any count of students, and any average of a student-level field, is now wrong.

The fix is grain, not deletion

  1. Aggregate the aid data to one row per student first (sum the award amounts), then join.
  2. Or use a relationship and let Tableau aggregate at the right level for each view.
Explicitly required

Do not delete records to make totals match. Removing rows until a number looks right destroys the analysis and is the specific failure this part of the exercise is testing for.

One more thing to catch: roughly one student in eight has no aid record at all. Join type matters here for the same reason it did with the program lookup.

Module 3 · Step 1 of 8

Row-level versus aggregate

Tableau Desktop · 25 min

This distinction causes more wrong numbers than any other calculation topic. Build both and compare.

Connect mvu_student_dim.csv. Create a calculated field named Aid per Credit (row):

[total_aid_awarded] / [credits_attempted]

This computes per student, then Tableau aggregates the results. Now the aggregate version, Aid per Credit (agg):

SUM([total_aid_awarded]) / SUM([credits_attempted])

Put college on Rows and both fields on Columns. Set the first to Average.

Why they differ

The first is an average of ratios — every student counts the same regardless of how many credits they took. The second is a ratio of sums, so students with heavier loads pull it further. Neither is wrong. Which one you want depends on whether your question is about the typical student or the typical dollar.

The error you'll hit

"Cannot mix aggregate and non-aggregate arguments" means you wrote something like SUM([a]) / [b]. Everything inside one calculation must sit at the same level. Wrap the loose field in an aggregation, or take the whole thing down to row level.

Module 3 · Step 2 of 8

Conditional logic: academic standing

Tableau Desktop · 20 min

Continuous measures are hard to talk about. Cohorts are easy. Connect mvu_term_gpa.csv and create Academic Standing:

IF [term_gpa] >= 3.70 THEN "Excellent"
ELSEIF [term_gpa] >= 3.00 THEN "Good standing"
ELSEIF [term_gpa] >= 2.00 THEN "Academic warning"
ELSE "Probation"
END

Drag it to Rows and a distinct count of student_id to Columns.

Tableau evaluates ELSEIF branches in order and stops at the first match, so ordering thresholds from high to low matters. Reverse them and everything lands in the first bucket.

Then argue about the cutoffs

3.0 is conventional for graduate probation, but you chose it, not the data. Every threshold in a calculated field is an editorial decision wearing a formula's clothing. Say so in your documentation.

Module 3 · Step 3 of 8

Date calculations: measuring a gap

Tableau Desktop · 15 min

One idea on this page: DATEDIFF is subtraction for dates. Two dates go in, a number comes out, in whatever unit you ask for. Nothing about it is subtle.

Relate mvu_student_dim.csv to mvu_enrollment_fact.csv on student_id — you need both, because the two dates live in different files.

FieldLives inMeaning
entry_datestudent_dimThe day the student started their programme
term_start_dateenrollment_factThe day that term began

Create a calculated field named Months Since Entry:

DATEDIFF('month', [entry_date], [term_start_date])

Read it left to right: count the months from the student's entry date to this term's start date. A student's first term returns 0. Their term a year later returns 12.

Why this is worth building

The 2020 and 2022 cohorts started two years apart, so comparing them by calendar date tells you nothing useful. Converted to months since entry, every student's first term is month 0 — and a 2020 student at month 12 is directly comparable to a 2022 student at month 12.

Put Months Since Entry on Columns and average term GPA on Rows, with cohort_year on Colour. All three cohorts now start at the same origin.

Sense-check every derived number

Drag the field to Text and check its range. Values should run from 0 to about 29 — the span of the data. A negative value means the two date fields are the wrong way round in the formula. A value in the thousands means a date is still typed as text and Tableau is counting days, not months.

That's the whole step. The next one introduces a different idea, so stop here and make sure this one works before moving on.

Module 3 · Step 4 of 8

Level of detail: one value per student

Tableau Desktop · 20 min

This page has one idea too, and the fastest way to understand it is to watch the problem happen before you see the fix.

Step 1 — Build the broken version on purpose

New worksheet. Put student_id on Rows and term_start_date on Text, set to Maximum. You get each student's last term. Correct so far.

Now drag college onto Rows as well.

The numbers changed. Tableau didn't compute "the last term per student" — it computed the maximum over whatever the view was grouping. Add a dimension, change the grouping, change the answer.

This is the default behaviour, not a bug

Ordinary aggregations are computed over whatever is currently in the view. That's usually what you want. It becomes a problem the moment you need a value that belongs to the student rather than to the view.

Step 2 — Pin it with FIXED

Create a calculated field named Last Term Attended:

{ FIXED [student_id] : MAX([term_start_date]) }

Read the braces as an instruction to Tableau: ignore what's on screen — group by student_id, and give me the maximum term date within each one.

PartWhat it says
{ }This is a level-of-detail expression
FIXEDUse the grain I name, not the view's
[student_id]The grain: one value per student
:Separator — read it as "compute"
MAX([term_start_date])What to compute within each student

Put the new field in the view and add or remove college again. It holds. That stability is the entire reason LODs exist.

Step 3 — Now combine what you have

You have a way to measure a gap between two dates, and a way to pin a value to one per student. Put them together for time to degree:

DATEDIFF('month', [entry_date], [Last Term Attended])

Use your saved Last Term Attended field rather than pasting the braces inside the DATEDIFF. It does the same thing and stays readable — and when something breaks, you can check each half separately.

Filter to status = "Graduated" and put the new field on Text as an Average.

Checkpoint

Median months from entry to final term, for graduates only (whole number)?

Show the expected answer

12

Filter status to Graduated first — without that filter you're including students still enrolled and those who withdrew early. Full-time graduates average about 13 months, part-time about 19.

Module 3 · Step 5 of 8

String calculations

Tableau Desktop · 15 min

The term code packs two facts into one string. Pull them apart.

LEFT([term_code], 4)

That's the calendar year. And the season:

IF RIGHT([term_code], 2) = "FA" THEN "Fall" ELSE "Spring" END

Now something more useful — an academic year, where Fall 2020 and Spring 2021 belong together:

IF RIGHT([term_code], 2) = "FA"
THEN LEFT([term_code], 4) + "-" + RIGHT(STR(INT(LEFT([term_code], 4)) + 1), 2)
ELSE STR(INT(LEFT([term_code], 4)) - 1) + "-" + RIGHT([term_code], 2)
END

Nested string functions get unreadable fast. Add a description to the field (right-click → Default Properties → Comment) so the next person — probably you, in March — can follow it.

Module 3 · Step 6 of 8

Formatting and default properties

Tableau Desktop · 15 min

A rate rendered as 0.5379 is a number nobody can read at a glance. Fix it once, at the field, rather than on every worksheet.

Create Graduation Rate:

AVG([is_graduated])

Then right-click the field → Default Properties → Number Format → Percentage, one decimal place.

Every view you build from now on inherits that format. While you're there, set a default aggregation and write a comment describing what the field means.

Why AVG of a 0/1 flag gives you a rate

The average of a column of ones and zeros is the proportion of ones. It's the cleanest way to compute a rate in Tableau, and it aggregates correctly at every level of detail without you writing anything further.

Checkpoint

Overall graduation rate across all 1,400 students, as a percentage (one decimal)?

Show the expected answer

Module 3 · Step 7 of 8

The cohort trap

Tableau Desktop · 20 min

Take the graduation rate you just built and split it by cohort_year.

The 2022 bar sits at zero.

Stop and work out why before you read on. Is that a fact about the 2022 cohort, or a fact about the dataset?

Show the answer

The data ends in Spring 2023. The 2022 cohort had two terms to complete a programme that takes four or more. Nobody could have graduated. The bar reports the boundary of data collection, not student performance.

Two defensible fixes: restrict the view to cohorts that have had time to finish, or redefine the measure as "graduated within N terms of entry" so every cohort is judged on the same window.

The transferable skill

Every dataset has an edge like this. Reporting the 2022 bar as a finding would be technically accurate and completely wrong — and the chart gives you no warning whatsoever. Noticing is the skill; Tableau is just the tool.

Module 4 · Step 1 of 7

Review: are you ready for Module 4?

Tableau Desktop · 25 min · do this before continuing

Module 4 assumes everything from the first three modules. Table calculations sit on top of a view you've already built correctly — if the underlying model or calculation is wrong, the table calculation will be confidently wrong too, and much harder to debug.

Work through this page before you continue. It should take about twenty-five minutes. If any part of it stalls you, the link beside it takes you back to the step that covers it.

Part A — Answer these without opening Tableau

Say each answer out loud or write it down before you open the box. Recognising an answer is not the same as being able to produce one.

1. What is the difference between a relationship and a join, and when does it matter?

A relationship keeps each table at its own grain and aggregates each one appropriately for whatever is in the view. A join flattens tables into one wide table, repeating rows from the smaller side. It matters any time you count or average something from the smaller table — student counts, averages of student-level fields, or any total that should not be multiplied by the number of related records.

Revisit: Relationships versus joins

2. Why does cleaning have to happen before deduplicating?

Duplicate detection compares strings exactly. Two records that differ only in capitalisation or trailing whitespace are different strings, so they survive a duplicate removal. Standardise the field first, then deduplicate.

Revisit: Deduplicate — in the right order

3. When do an average of ratios and a ratio of sums give different answers?

Whenever the denominator varies across records. The average of ratios weights every record equally; the ratio of sums weights records by size. The gap widens as your records become more unequal. Neither is wrong — they answer different questions.

Revisit: Row-level versus aggregate

4. What does a level-of-detail expression do that a normal calculation cannot?

It computes at a grain you specify rather than the grain of the view, and holds that grain even when the view's dimensions change. Use it when you need a per-entity value — a first date, a maximum, an entity total — inside a view that is broken out by something else.

Revisit: Date calculations

5. Why did the 2022 cohort show a zero percent graduation rate?

The data ends in Spring 2023. That cohort had two terms to complete a multi-year programme, so nobody could have graduated. The chart is reporting the boundary of data collection, not student performance.

Revisit: The cohort trap

Module 4 · Step 2 of 7

Worksheet 1 — build the base view

Tableau Desktop · 15 min

Table calculations behave differently from everything you've built so far. A calculated field is computed in the data source, before the view exists. A table calculation is computed after the query returns, using only what is actually in the view.

That means the same table calculation gives different answers depending on what you've put on Rows and Columns. Build the view first, deliberately.

  1. Connect mvu_enrollment_fact.csv.
  2. college to Rows.
  3. term_code to Columns.
  4. A record count to Text — see below for where to find it.
Where the record count lives

If you're looking for a field called Number of Records, it no longer exists. Tableau removed it in version 2020.2. Each table now carries its own count instead. Use whichever of these you prefer:

  • In the Data pane, scroll to the bottom of the table's field list and drag mvu_enrollment_fact (Count) into the view. This is the direct replacement.
  • Or drag any field that is never null — student_id works — and set its aggregation to Count. Not Count (Distinct): that counts students, not records.
  • Or create a calculated field containing just 1 and aggregate it with SUM. This is the old behaviour, rebuilt by hand.

All three give the same number. The first is fastest; the second is worth knowing because it makes the distinction between Count and Count (Distinct) visible.

You now have a text table: six terms across, six colleges down. Every number in it is an enrolment count. This grid is what the table calculation will operate on.

Note the shape of the numbers. Fall terms are roughly double the spring terms, because a new cohort enters each fall. That pattern matters in two steps' time.

Worksheet 1 — addressing and partitioning

Tableau Desktop · 25 min

Right-click the measure pill on the Marks card → Add Table Calculation. Set the type to Running Total.

Leave Compute Using at Table (across). Each college's row accumulates left to right across terms, and each row restarts at its own first term.

The two words that matter

TermMeaning
AddressingThe dimension the calculation moves along. Here, term.
PartitioningThe dimension the calculation restarts within. Here, college.

Now cycle through the Compute Using options and watch the numbers move:

  • Table (across) — accumulates across terms, restarting per college. Sensible.
  • Table (down) — accumulates down colleges within each term. Almost certainly meaningless here.
  • Pane (down) — same, confined to each pane.

Finally choose Specific Dimensions and tick term_code only, leaving college unticked. That states the relationship explicitly: address along term, partition by college. It produces the same result as Table (across) here — but it will keep producing the right result if someone later rearranges the view.

Finally, add the grand total the exercise asks for: Analysis → Totals → Show Column Grand Totals. That gives you the number your checkpoint compares against.

Why this matters more than it looks

Table (across) is defined by the visual layout. Specific Dimensions is defined by the data. Swap two pills on a view built with Table (across) and your running total silently starts accumulating along the wrong axis. Nothing errors. The numbers just quietly become wrong.

Ask the harder question

A moving average across fall and spring terms averages two genuinely different things into one number. The volatility you removed was real information about how the academic year works. Smoothing didn't reveal a trend — it concealed a pattern.

Smoothing is legitimate when the fluctuation is noise. It's misleading when the fluctuation is structure. Here it's structure, and the correct fix is to compare like periods rather than average across unlike ones.

Module 4 · Step 6 of 7

Worksheet 3 — a parameter, several metrics

Tableau Desktop · 25 min

A parameter is a single user-controlled value. On its own it does nothing at all — it only matters once a calculated field reads it.

  1. Right-click in the Data pane → Create → Parameter.
  2. Name it Select Metric, data type String.
  3. Allowable values → List. Enter: Headcount, Graduation Rate, Average Aid.

Now the calculated field that reads it. Connect mvu_student_dim.csv and create Dynamic Measure:

CASE [Select Metric]
WHEN "Headcount"       THEN COUNTD([student_id])
WHEN "Graduation Rate" THEN AVG([is_graduated])
WHEN "Average Aid"     THEN AVG([total_aid_awarded])
END

Right-click the parameter → Show Parameter. Put college on Rows and Dynamic Measure on Columns, then switch metrics from the dropdown.

Every branch must return the same data type

All three branches above return numbers, which is what makes the CASE valid. Mixing a number and a string across branches fails. So does mixing an aggregate with a non-aggregate — the same discipline from Module 3 applies here.

The honest limitation

One axis, three metrics on wildly different scales — a headcount in the hundreds, a rate between 0 and 1, a dollar amount in the thousands. The axis rescales, but the number format can't follow. This is the standard trade-off of dynamic measures, and naming it is better than pretending it isn't there.

Checkpoint

1400

Every student appears once. Graduation Rate should read about 0.538 overall. If Headcount shows 10,147 you are counting enrolment rows rather than students — check that the branch uses COUNTD.

Fixed size first. Designing at Automatic means every element shifts as you resize the window, and you never quite know what your reader will see.

Build the frame before you add content

  2. Drag a Horizontal container into the top of it. This row will hold two views side by side.
  3. Drag Headcount by College into the horizontal container.
  4. Drag Withdrawal by Intensity in beside it.
  5. Drag Graduation Rate by Cohort into the lower half of the vertical container.

Then tidy it

  1. Select the outer container → its dropdown arrow → Distribute Contents Evenly.
  2. Select each view → Layout tab on the left → set Outer Padding to 4 on all sides.
  3. Delete any legend that isn't telling the reader something they can't get from the axis.
Why containers matter

You can drag views straight onto the canvas as floating objects, and for about five minutes it feels faster. Then you resize something and everything else stays put, overlapping and misaligned. Containers hold relationships between objects; floating objects hold only coordinates. Build the frame first and the layout maintains itself.

Make it interactive

Three views sitting together is a poster. A filter action makes it a tool.

  2. Name it something readable: College selection.

Module 5 · Step 4 of 6

What did you leave out?

Tableau Desktop · 15 min

This step has no new mechanics. It's the one that separates a dashboard from a pile of charts.

Look at what you've built and take things away:

  • Every legend that repeats what an axis already says.
  • Gridlines that aren't helping anyone read a value.
  • Titles that restate the obvious. Students by College above a chart of students by college is a wasted line.
  • Any view that doesn't serve the dashboard's single question.
  • Decimal places nobody needs. 53.8% is fine; 53.79% is noise.

Then answer this in one sentence, for yourself: what question does this dashboard answer in thirty seconds?

The test that matters

If you can't name the question, your reader certainly can't. A dashboard that answers three questions adequately is usually worse than one that answers a single question well — because the reader has to work out which one you meant.

Add a Text object from the Objects panel and write the question at the top of the dashboard. If it feels redundant, that's a good sign — it means the layout is already communicating it.

Story points

Tableau Desktop · 25 min

A dashboard lets a reader explore. A story tells them what you found. Different jobs, and the difference matters: a story removes the reader's freedom in exchange for clarity.

  3. Click the caption box above the view and replace the placeholder with a sentence stating your headline finding.
  4. Click Blank to add story point 2. Drag the dashboard in again, then apply a filter — part-time only, or a single cohort.

Origin-destination flows

Tableau Desktop · 20 min

New worksheet. One calculation draws a line between two spatial points.

Commute Line = MAKELINE([Home Point], [Campus Point])
  1. Drag Commute Line onto the canvas.
  2. Drag student_id to Detail so each line renders separately. Without this you get one merged shape.
  3. Drag campus_name to Color.
  4. Click Color and drop opacity to roughly 30%, so overlapping corridors are readable rather than a solid block.

Main and Riverside produce tight fans. Online produces long lines reaching across Texas and out to Chicago and Phoenix.

The third trap in a row, and the same one

MAKELINE draws a line whether or not anyone travels it. Those long Online lines represent enrolment relationships, not journeys. Nobody drives from Chicago to Austin for a class that happens online.

A flow map implies movement. When the movement isn't real, the chart is a visual fiction — competently built, professionally rendered, and false. Either exclude that campus or relabel the chart to say what it actually shows.
